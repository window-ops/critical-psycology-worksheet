Critical Psychology Worksheet

Tracing Personal Thoughts to Systemic Causes: A Step-by-Step Non-Professional Guided Analysis

What is Critical Psychology?

Critical Psychology is an approach that goes beyond individual thoughts and behaviors to examine how social, economic, and political systems shape personal experiences, emotions, and mental health. It challenges traditional psychology by exploring the root causes of distress in power structures, inequality, and systemic oppression.

Unlike conventional therapies like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), which focus on identifying and reframing negative thoughts on an individual level (e.g., “Change your thought to feel better”), Critical Psychology asks deeper questions such as:

  • Why do these thoughts arise in the first place?
  • What social or economic systems might be responsible for creating and maintaining these thoughts or feelings?
  • How do societal norms, cultural values, and economic pressures influence individual mental health?

By uncovering these systemic roots, Critical Psychology seeks not only personal healing but also social change. It encourages individuals to develop critical awareness of the world around them and to challenge the conditions that cause psychological distress.

Advantages, Disadvantages and Limitations Relative to CBT

Advantages of Critical Psychology Compared to CBT

  • Broader Contextual Understanding: Critical Psychology situates mental health issues within social, political, and economic contexts, providing a more comprehensive understanding of distress beyond individual cognition.
  • Addresses Root Causes: Rather than solely modifying thoughts and behaviors, Critical Psychology questions the origins of distress in societal structures, aiming for long-term transformation.
  • Holistic Approach: By integrating societal, cultural, and economic factors, it aligns mental health with broader human rights and community well-being.

Disadvantages of Critical Psychology Compared to CBT

  • Less Structured and Standardized: Critical Psychology lacks the well-defined protocols and evidence-based manuals typical of CBT, which can make it harder to measure effectiveness and replicate results.
  • May Not Provide Immediate Symptom Relief: Since it targets systemic change and critical consciousness, it might not offer the rapid symptom reduction that CBT often provides through cognitive restructuring.
  • Requires High Client Engagement and Awareness: The approach demands clients engage with complex social theories and activism, which may not suit everyone’s immediate needs or readiness.

Limitations of Critical Psychology

  • Applicability Limited to Systemic Issues: Critical Psychology is most effective when the problem has a clear systemic or structural root. It primarily addresses negative thoughts and distress related to social structures, economic conditions, and material concerns.
  • Less Effective for Interpersonal or Relationship Issues: It is not designed to directly treat relationship problems or interpersonal disorders, which often require more focused, individual or dyadic therapeutic approaches.
  • Complex and Difficult to Understand: The approach relies on multiple theories part of the critical tradition, making it intellectually demanding and potentially overwhelming for both practitioners and clients.
  • Potential for Bias: As with any theoretical framework, Critical Psychology carries inherent biases—particularly towards societal and systemic explanations of distress. This may limit its objectivity, much like CBT’s bias toward individualism.
  • Challenges in Practical Implementation: The complexity and scope of Critical Psychology can make it harder to apply consistently in clinical settings, requiring specialized training and significant client readiness for critical reflection and activism.

For each example, we start with a personal thought or feeling (e.g., anxiety, fear, negative self-belief). Then we follow the “Why?” chain step-by-step to uncover the deeper social, economic, or political roots of that thought. Finally, we identify possible systemic solutions.

Example 1: Anxiety About Beauty Standards

Step Analysis
1. I believe I don’t look good.
2. Why? Because I fear people will hate me for it.
3. Why? Because of prevailing beauty standards.
4. Why? Because beauty standards are socially constructed.
5. Why? Because the economic system profits from beauty industries.
6. Why? Because capitalism exploits insecurities to maximize profit.
Solution: Challenge beauty norms, support media diversity, address capitalist exploitation of insecurities.
